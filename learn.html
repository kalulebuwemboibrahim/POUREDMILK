THE CAKE LAB

Learn what
the batter is doing.

Baking becomes less mysterious when you can connect an ingredient or technique to the structure you see in the finished cake.

Why cakes rise

Cake volume comes from gases expanding inside a structure that can hold them. Those gases may come from beaten eggs, chemical leaveners such as baking powder, steam, or a combination.

As the batter heats, trapped air expands and water turns to steam. Proteins and starches set gradually, turning a fluid batter into a crumb that can support the bubbles.

Why cakes sink

A cake can collapse when its structure has not set strongly enough to hold the gases produced during baking. Underbaking is a common cause, but excess liquid, too much leavener, weak structure or aggressive temperature changes can contribute too.

The practical lesson is simple: a cake needs enough lift to expand, but enough structure to hold that expansion.

Flour & gluten

Wheat flour contains proteins that can form gluten when hydrated and mixed. Gluten is useful because it creates a network, but cake generally needs less gluten development than bread.

That is why cake mixing is usually restrained after flour goes in. More mixing means more network development and, potentially, a tougher crumb.

Eggs in cake

Eggs are unusually versatile. Their proteins set with heat, their yolks bring fat and emulsifiers, and beaten whites can trap air. In some cakes, eggs are the main structural system; in others they are one supporting ingredient among many.

Oven temperature

Heat changes cake in stages: fats melt, gases expand, water moves as steam, proteins coagulate and starches gelatinize. Too little heat can leave a weak structure; too much can set or brown the outside before the centre catches up.

TECHNIQUE

The moves that
change the crumb.

CREAMING

Build air before flour arrives.

Beating softened butter with sugar creates tiny air pockets. Eggs and flour later stabilize that structure.

FOLDING

Protect the air you made.

Use broad, deliberate folds when working with whipped eggs or delicate batters. Stop when the last dry streak disappears.

MIXING

Enough is usually enough.

Once flour is hydrated, extra mixing can develop more gluten than a tender cake wants.

DONENESS

Read the cake.

Look for colour, spring-back, a set centre and a clean or slightly moist skewer. The clock is only one clue.
